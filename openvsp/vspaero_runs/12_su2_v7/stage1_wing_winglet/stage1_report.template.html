That is a thin margin, but it is where transonic aircraft are supposed to sit — cruising short of the knee wastes speed already paid for in sweep and thickness. The concern is not the margin today; it is that fuselage and nacelles usually push drag rise down in Mach, and there is no slack to absorb that in stages 2 and 3.

Why the drag rises

Splitting each polar into its two parts answers this, and the answer is not the obvious one. Zero-lift drag — the part that depends on how thick the wing is — barely moves: 12.5 to 15.6 counts across the entire subcritical range. What collapses is span efficiency, from 0.963 down to 0.721.

So the shock is not forming because the wing is too fat. It forms because of how hard the upper surface is working to make lift. At cruise the arithmetic breaks down like this:

true induced drag   CL² / (π · 0.963 · AR)  =  48.1 counts
lost to compressibility   same, at e = 0.721  →  +16.2 counts
zero-lift   CD0 at M0.80  =  15.6 counts (≈3.5 of which is numerical noise)
total                                    79.8 counts

That is actionable. The lever is upper-surface shaping and cruise lift, not thinning the wing. It also means flying lighter or higher pays off more than proportionally.

About that cruise lift of 0.36

It is low, and that is worth understanding rather than accepting. It is not a choice — weight and altitude force it:

wing loading   11,660 lb / 193.678 ft²  =  60.2 lb/ft²
dynamic pressure   FL410, M0.80  =  167.2 lb/ft²
cruise CL   60.2 / 167.2  =  0.360

Transport aircraft typically cruise nearer CL 0.5–0.6. This wing loading is roughly half theirs at comparable dynamic pressure, which is another way of saying the wing is large for the weight it carries — almost certainly the stall-speed requirement (Vs ≈ 91 kt) driving area, then that area having to fly around all day at cruise.

What this says about wing size — and what it does not

The large wing costs cruise efficiency: config.md puts best L/D at CL ≈ 0.853 against an actual cruise L/D of 12.3–12.5 versus a possible 16.9. Lift does govern shock strength — an earlier sweep run at CL 0.61 by mistake gave 259 counts at M0.80 instead of 80, with apparent drag divergence down at M0.74. Same wing, same mesh, only the lift differed.

But re-evaluating the fitted polars at higher lift shows the drag-divergence Mach is far less sensitive than that comparison suggests: a 20% smaller wing (CL 0.45) moves it only 0.809 → 0.803. Drag level climbs steeply; the knee barely shifts. So there is no strong transonic argument for keeping the wing large.

This campaign cannot settle wing sizing. The prize for a smaller wing is reduced skin friction, which is viscous and therefore invisible to an inviscid solve. These results show only the penalty of shrinking (induced drag rising as span comes in), never the benefit. The trade needs a real parasite-drag figure for the complete aircraft — which is what stages 2 and 3 exist to produce.

What not to trust

  • The CD0 and e columns above M0.80. Watch CD0 go 15.6 → 13.1 → 114 counts. Zero-lift drag cannot fall then leap tenfold. Past drag divergence the flow stops following a lift-squared law, so the fit shovels wave drag into whichever term will take it. The total CD is unaffected — that is interpolated between real solved points, not model-dependent.
  • Everything is inviscid. No skin friction, no boundary layer, no separation. Real drag is higher; this describes pressure and wave behaviour only.
  • Wing and winglet only. No fuselage, no engine, no tail. Interference drag — the whole reason the over-wing engine layout needs checking — is not in these numbers.
  • The noise floor is about 3.5 counts, measured as leftover drag at M0.60 where inviscid theory says there should be essentially none. Treat differences smaller than that as nothing.
How this data was nearly wrong

The first complete sweep ran at a fixed 3.7° angle of attack, a value inherited from VSPAERO, a vortex-lattice code that does not model M0.80 compressibility properly. It reported CL 0.34 at that angle; Euler gives 0.608. Holding angle fixed also lets lift climb with Mach (0.42 → 0.66), which manufactures a drag rise that is partly just the lift increasing.

Every number on this page comes from holding lift constant and letting angle vary, which is what the aircraft actually does.

Stage 2 attempted, and stopped

Stage 2 (fuselage, belly fairing, wing/winglet, vertical/dorsal/horizontal tail — no nacelle or pylon yet) was attempted. Three real defects were found and fixed along the way, each a genuine bug rather than a tuning problem:

  • The dorsal fin's fixed root chord overlaps the (resizable) vertical tail root — harmless as crossing thin panels in the VLM analysis this project used before, but it crashed OpenVSP's own solid mesh engine outright the first time this geometry went through a real 3-D mesher. Dropped from the CFD geometry; confirmed as the cause by re-running without it.
  • The T-tail junction (horizontal tail root meeting the swept vertical tail tip) formed a patch with a boundary shape the mesher couldn't flatten into a 2-D parameter space. Confirmed real 3-D surface, not a fake flat cap — fixed by loosening how aggressively the mesher groups triangles into patches.
  • OpenVSP's own far-field-box triangulation proved unreliable in several different ways on this bigger model. Fixed architecturally: stop trusting it, strip it out entirely, and rebuild that one boundary face by hand with exact geometry instead.

Where it's actually blocked: a genuine overlapping-facet defect — two triangles folded flat against each other, essentially a zero-degree fold — inside a large merged surface spanning the fuselage belly, the wing's lower surface, and the belly fairing together. This traces to something the project's own design notes already predicted before any of this CFD work began: the belly fairing meets the fuselage as a hard boolean join, not a smooth blend, and a sharp join like that was flagged as likely to cause exactly this kind of problem the first time it hit a real solver. This is that prediction landing.

Decision: stop here for now. Stage 1 already answers the load-bearing question this campaign exists for — the wing's transonic behavior at the lift it actually flies at — and that answer stands on its own regardless of what happens with the rest of the aircraft. Stage 2 and Stage 3 (critically including the OTWEM nacelle installation penalty, still the single most important unanswered question for this configuration) remain open for a future pass, most likely needing the fairing/fuselage junction re-lofted as a real blend before the mesh will close.
