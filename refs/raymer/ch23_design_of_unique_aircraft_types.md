9 Forward -Swe pt Wing 
The forward-swept wing (FSW) offers the same transonic and supersonic 
drag reduction as an aft-swept wing and has an added benefit in stall. For an 
aft-swept wing the spanwise flow resulting from the sweep causes bounda rylayer thickening and sepa ration out at the tips, leading to early tip stall. For 
the forward- swept wing, this phenomenon affects the roots instead, leaving 
the tips with good airflow up to high angles of attack, improving maximum 
lift and retaining aileron control. 
Unfortunately, there is a fundam ental problem with FSW- aeroelastic 
divergence. Under certairi. circumstances, the wing breaks off. 
Regardless of sweep direction, a wing with conventiona l structural will, 
under a lift load, bend about an axis perpendicular to the direction of the 
wing box structure. This is illustrated in the top example of Fig. 23 .4, 
where this perpendicular direction is indicated by lines on the wing box. In 
effect, the wing bends as if those lines are hinges. This is no problem for 
an aft-swept wing. 
When a forward-swept wing bends upward about those "hing es," the tips 
are deflected such that they experience a higher angle of attack. This 
increases lift, causing more bend ing, causing a higher tip angle of attack. 
At some aircraft speed, the wing will "diverge" and break. The structure 
must be strong enough that this divergence occurs at a speed higher than 
the aircraft's maximum placard speed, often resulting in a substantial 
weight pen alty.

with minimal weight pena lty. First, composi tes are typically stiffer than

Asymme tric Airplane s 
Most aircraft design is done assuming a plane of symmetry-we usua lly 
design just the left half of the aircraft and assume that the other side is a 
mirror image. This simplifies design, but even more impor tant, it simplifies 
lateral- directional stability and handling qualit ies. Spec ifically, with a 
symmetric design we can usua lly assume that the rolling and yawing 
moment s are zero when the aircraft is at zero sideslip angle regardl ess of 
angle of attack, and that these moments increment the same whether a 
right or left sideslip occurs. 
This is genera lly true for gliders and jets, although the asymmetric formation of vortices on the nose is always a potential problem (Chapter 8). 
For propeller-p owered aircraft, this assum ption is often untrue or irrelevant. 
A tracto r-propeller aircraft is flying in its own prop wash, and that propwash includes a rotational motion. Thus, the aircraft can have a symmetric 
flowfield only if it has multiple propellers that rotate in oppo site directions 
on opposi te sides of the aircraft. Otherwise, a physically symmetric 
prop-powered aircraft flies in a nonsymmetric flowfield, so that it acts like a 
nonsymmetric aircraft. Furthermore, the p-effect described in Chapter 16 
includes the fact that the center of thrust of a propeller moves laterally 
when the disk of the propeller is at an angle of attack to the flow, as during a 
climb. This causes a yawing moment, even for a symmetric aircraft. 
Most designers, faced with these problems, design a physically symmetric 
aircraft that will fly symmetrically when power is turned off and then try to fix 
these power-induced effects with trim and by using tricks such as angling the 
vertical tail or the engine thrust axis. Other designers have reasoned, "since it 
isn't going to fly symmetrically anyway, why does it have to look symmetri c?" 
They have even thought of ways to use nons ymmetric geo metry to make it fly 
more symmetrically! 
An early example was the German Blohm-Voss Bv- 141, flown in 1938 
(Fig. 23.9) . By placing the pilot and tail gunner in a pod offset from the 
main fuselage containing the engine and tails, the gunner was afforded a 
clear field of fire while the pilot got unsu rpassed downward visibil ity for a 
single-engine attack aircraft. The p-effect moved the thrust axis to the 
right, closer to the centerline, and made the flying characteristics far more 
symmetrical than the aircraft's appearance would indicate. 
Figure 23. 10 shows Burt Rutan's Boomerang. While the designer of the 
Bv-141 kept a symmetrical wing and carefully balanced the aircraft masses 
on each side of its center, the Boomerang is so wildly nonsymmetric that 
one suspects Rutan was ju st showing off, until you follow through his logic 
and realize that everything on the design has a reason. 
First, Boom erang had to be twin-en gine for safety, but the actual statistics 
indicate that a twin is no safer than a single-en gine aircraft. Although having 
an extra engine can save you if you lose an engine over the Alps at midnight, 
you also get a doubled risk of having one engine fail. In a conventional design

Some Mor e Inno vative Wings 
Aircraft innovators seek improvements in all parts of the aircraft but 
seem to focus espe cially upon the wing. Their "great ideas" include better airfoils, new wing-tip shapes, exotic winglets, and exciting, wild wing arrangements. Perhaps this makes sense: the wing is what sets the airplane apart 
from other vehicles, and the wing generates all of the lift and causes much 
of the drag and weight. Of course, it should draw the attention of innovators, 
but sometimes it seems that they have gone a little too far!

Another variant of the asymmetric design offers the theoretical minimum 
drag, maximum efficiency supersonic design. The oblique wing is a swept 
wing with one side swept forward and the other swept to the rear, that is, 
a single straight wing skewed with respect to the fuselage. This is usually 
pivoted at the center to allow variable sweep, which for this concept is easy 
to mechanize with little weight pena lty. 
Because the wing structural box is straight, the oblique wing is much 
lighter in weight than other swept wings. It obtains the same transonic and 
supersonic benefits as other swept wings and is no worse than the aft-swept

The active aeroelastic wing (AA W) was developed at Rockwell International* to solve problems with excessi ve flexibility in Rockwell's Advanced 
Tactical Fighter design (Fig. 7.2). AA W was first conc eived as a comp uterized 
implementation of a portion of every B-47 pilot's training. The B-47, with its 
swept high-aspect- ratio wings, had such bad aileron roll reversal (see Chapter 
16) that flight was normally restricted to below 455 kt {840 km/h} . Above 
that speed, B-47 pilots practiced flying the airplane "backwards," moving 
the stick to the right to roll the bomber to the left. AA W does this, and 
much more. 
In the active aeroelastic wing the wing box is deliber ately designed with 
reduced torsional stiffness, while of normal streng th in all other direct ions. 
This saves weight because torsional stiffness requireme nts are often the 
most critical loads on the outer third of the wing. High-speed actuators are 
used on leading- and trail ing- edge control surfac es. These are capable of 
deflecting up as well as down. The whole thing is controlled with a computerized control system, which deflects those surfaces as required. 
For roll control, the trailing-ed ge surfaces deflect like normal ailerons at 
low speeds, creating lift on that side of the wing (Fig. 23. 18 a) . The illustration 
shows the AA W surfaces on the left wing of a notional advanced transport, so 
this would roll the airplane to the right. At higher speeds the ailerons twist 
the wing so much that the net force is downwards (Fig. 23.18 b), rolling the 
airplane to the left. Downward deflection of the leading- edge flaps produces 
a download at the front of the wing box, twisting the wing leading edge down 
even more (Fig. 23.1 8c) . . 
AA W even allows the creation of additional wing-tip drag for yaw control 
and to act as speed brakes. This is done by deflecting the leading- and 
trailing-edge surfaces so that they "fight" each other, each trying to twist 
the wing in the oppo site direction. As shown (Fig. 23.18 e), this would yaw 
the airplane to the left. 
AA W is often compared to the wing warping in the Wright Flyer, but 
that was accomplished mechanica lly, with control wires pulled by the pilot. 
Another compa rison is to the servotab flight control systems used by 
airplanes as large and fast as the Boeing 707, where the ailerons had no 
mechanical actuat ion. Instead, the control system moved a small tab 
surface at the back of the aileron. This moved the aileron, and the aileron 
rolled the airplane. AA W is wing warping, mechanized with servotabs.

It would be difficult to imagine flying from Los Angeles to Chicago at a

designed like a flying boat but intended to keep within ground effect offers 
fantastic L/ D. Called "wing- in-ground- effect" (WIG), such designs were 
built and flown by Lippisch and others, most notably the Russians. They 
call such aircraft "Ekrano -planes" and have built huge ones, both propeller

During the Cold War, satellite photos detected something immense flying 
very fast over the Caspian Sea, which was soon nicknamed the "Caspian Sea

water in front of the wing to create an air cushion for added lift (a technique 
called power- augmented ram or PAR). The KM has two more turboj ets 
mounted on the vertical tail for extra thrust during takeoff and was said to 
cruise at 230 kt {430 km/h}. Unfortunately, the only KM ever built crashed 
during takeoff in 19 80.

The desi gn of a derivative of an existing airplane is proba bly the lea st 
innovative thing we designers are called upon to do, but sometimes it takes 
the most innovative thinking. The problem is simple: whatever we are 
trying to do with the modified airplane is constrained by the reality of the 
existing airplane. For a blank-sheet- of-paper design, if we need a little 
more wing span, we can redraw a few lines or type a few commands into

the CAD program. For a derivative design, we need to know if it is feasible 
and affordable to extend the wing span. 
Will the wing center section take the extra load? If not, it needs to be 
strengt hened, which will increase the cost. Does the extended wing tip 
strike the ground in a bad landing? If so, the landing gear legs might need 
lengthening which is nearly imposs ible for most airplanes. If the aircraft 
total weight increases, can the landing gear handle the loads and are the 
tires big enough? Is the rudder big eno ugh to handle the extra y awing 
moments? It goes on and on, and if some real-w orld constraint is ignored 
or forgotten, the proj ect fails. 
In fac t, you really cannot complete the concept ual design of an aircraft 
derivative until you've taken an in- depth look at some aspec ts that would 
normally be consi dered a detail design issue, like the stress calcula tions of 
the wing center section. There is a cost step function: it is fairly cheap to 
extend the wing span until you reach the leng th where the extra load requires 
a wing center-s ection redesign, and then the cost sudden ly goes up a lot. You 
need to know where that is before you can decide how much to extend the 
wing span. There are no top-level, rule-of -thumb estimat ions. You need to 
do the real calcula tions. 
Luck ily, most airplanes have some growth allowances built in. The 
landing gear of new aircraft are usua lly designed with a 25% increase in 
gross weight, to allow for future growth. There is sometimes extra volume 
in less accessible parts of the aircraft such as near the wing tips, which can 
be converted to fuel tanks if need be. A design might have extra ground clearance to allow for later retrofitting with a bigger propeller or larger turbofan . 
In some cases, a new design will delibera tely have an entire series of preplanned product impro vements (P3I) built in, with a clever configuration that 
allows a later fuselage stretch, or winglet addition, or re-en gining, etc. A 
similar process is called "spiral developmen t," a term from the software 
indu stry and implying a bit less preplanning but a continuous improvement 
process none theless. We pilots shudder at any use of the word "spiral" in connection with an aircraft -the phrase "death spiral" comes to mind! 
Certain aspects of an aircraft create extreme problems for derivative aircraft. It is very difficult to increase the allowable total aircraft weight for a 
given landing-gear system, once the built-in growth allowance is used up. 
The shock- struts need greater diameter. The strut itself needs to be stronger, 
and the strut forgings are both expensive and long-lead fabrication items. 
The tires cannot have their press ure raised to carry the greater load; that 
will damage the runways. If the tires are made larger, they won't fit inside 
the wheel wells. The brakes inside the tires need greater physical size and 
mass to stop the heavier airplane. 
Ground clearance can restrict propeller size or podded engine diameter. 
For an internal jet engine, it is almost impossible to increase the diameter of 
the internal inlet duct. An entire redesign is necess ary, as was done for the 
F-86H and the British Nimrod.

are forced to almost perfectly match the known capabi lities of the original 
airplane. Otherwise, nobod y will belie ve the numbers for the modified airplane. This often requires "tweaking" and "fudge- factoring" the analysis 
inputs until the correct answers are obtained. 
Once we have calibrated the analysis to the unmo dified aircraft and can 
match the range and key performance points, we can define the required 
changes to the geometry and est imate the revised aerod ynamics including 
the effects of increased wetted area, revised wing planform, and pos sibly 
component interferences. 
We must also estimate the impact of the design changes on the empty 
weight. This can be difficult, and our usual statistical equations might not 
give us the right answer. The weight of a modified part is greater than the 
weight of that part if designed all new. Often, a simple pou nds-per-sq uarefoot method is better than a sophisticated statistical equat ion. The best 
answer, though, is a detail weights buildup of the additional parts. 
If there is a change to propulsion, the new values for thrust and SFC must 
be estimated. Next, we calculate range and performance for the modified aircraft using the above methods. Hopeful ly, it all seems reasonable; if not, find 
out why!

The estimation of the cost of a derivative aircraft program is very difficult. 
You cannot estimate cost by analyzing the revised design as if it were new and 
then analyzing the cost of the original design using the same methods, then 
subtracting one from the other. This does not work! Not even the proven 
methods of Chapter 18 can be applied in this fashio n. 
The best cost estimation is through a detailed work-b reakdown- structure 
(WBS) buildup, detailing the actual tasks required to fly and produce the aircraft. This shou ld be done using actual data for the aircraft being modified, 
but this is difficult to obtain. 
A simple cost- estimation method for derivative aircraft design comes from 
the RAND Corpor ation, source of the DAPC A method of Chapter 18. A statistical analysis of various derivative proj ects was performed yielding straight 
lines on log-log graph paper. These can be used to estimate the labor hour s 
for the new parts of a derivative aircraft (see (16 9] ). While based only upon

classes of aircraft until better data are available. Take no credit for the old 
parts which are "thrown away." Nobod y reimburses you for those "returns. "
