When talking about elect ric motors and their power supplies we need to

Recall that in physics, "work" is a force acting along a distance, calculated 
as their product (foot-pou nds or newton-meters, called joules).

joules/s, called watts). The term "horsep ower" was actually defined by

engine. He estimated the continuous output of a horse as 550 ft-lb/s

recent studies have determined that the average power output of a healthy 
horse is, in fact, 1 hp.

Ele ctric Motor Basic s 
There are many options for aircraft elect ric propulsion. Elect ricity can be 
used to power a propeller, or a ducted fan, or even the compressor and/ or fan 
of a fuel-based turbine engine. The wildest conce pt, studied during the 1960s, 
uses electro hydrodynamic forces (ionic disch arge) to acce lerate air through a 
mesh, creating noiseless thrust from a device with no moving parts . Unfortunately, the elect rical power required to create this thrust is 10-2 0 times 
higher than for a large- diameter propeller. f56l 
In most applications the power used for flight comes from an electric 
motor. These are quiet, smoot h, and highly reliabl e. They usually weigh 
less than equivalent power gas motors, even including the motor controller 
normally required. 
The first working direct current (DC) electric motor goes back to 1827, 
and motors were in com mercial production a mere decade later. By 1888, 
elect ric motors were a proven technolog y, and the first electric trolleys 
were entering service. That same year eccentric genius Nikola Tesla developed a practical alternating current (AC) motor. 
The basic idea of an elect ric motor consists of a nonmoving magnetic 
object ("stato r"), rigged to pull a rotating magnetic object ("rotor") in a circular path (Fig. 20. 1). Magnets have north and south poles just like the Earth, 
which actua lly is a magnet. Oppo site poles attract, whereas similar poles 
repel. The rotor and stator have north and south poles on opposi te sides of

After the rotor has been pulled through a path of 180 deg, it would lock to 
the position closest to the pulling magnet so that the polarity of the pull is 
quickly reversed. The rotor then goes around another 180 deg, and the

Either the rotor or the stator can be permanent magnets (lodestone or 
magnetized metal), or either one can be electromagn ets, which are windings 
of wire through which a current is passed. At least one of them must be an 
electromagnet so that its polar ity can be reversed. 
While electric motors are classed as DC or AC by the type of electrical 
current they can accept; inside they are all "alterna ting curren t." The 
current flow must alternate to reverse magnetic pol arity to keep the rotor 
spinning around. In a DC motor, the "comm utator" switches the polar ity 
of the current flow to the rotor windings depending on the rotor angle. 
Originally this switching was done by mechanical "brushes," which can 
literally resemble paint brushes made of wire resting against a rotating electrical contact attached to the rotor. Brushes can also be metallic or graphite 
pads that are pressed against the contacts on the rotor. The current flow 
direction depends upon which con tacts on the rotor are touching the 
brushes as it rotates around. 
In modern "brushless" motors, electronic circuits sense the rotor position and switch the current flow. This impro ves efficiency and eliminates 
the brushes' high potential for sparking -a very bad thing espec ially in an 
airplane. 
The toy electric motors that we took apart as children are mostly DC, 
with permanent magnet stators outside and brushes supplying current to 
vary the polarity of the rotating electromagnets inside. This author had 
more success taking the111 apart than putting them back together. 
Electric motors for high-po wer applications more common ly have the 
current variation applied to electroma gnet stators, with the rotor bei ng 
either permanent magnets or electro magnets whose pola rity doesn't change. 
Also, practical motors often have the stators inside, with permanent 
magnets mounted to a "squirrel cage" rotor in a ring around the fixed 
stators. If you take apart that com puter in front of you, you'll prob ably find

Alternating current is just what it sounds like: the current flow const antly

equal to that cycle rate or a submu ltiple of it. If the rotor is made of electromagnets, the current can be passed to it through brushes press ing on co ncen tric 
rings. If the rotor uses permanent magnets, brushes are not required.

There is a problem with the "two-phase" motor described above, where 
the rotor gets pulled through a path of 18 0 deg and then the pola rity is 
revers ed. If the rotor is lined up so that the rotor magnet poles are closest 
to the stator magnet poles, the magnetic pull is lined up with the axis of 
rotation so that there is no moment arm, thus no moment, thus no rotation. 
The rotor is stuck. Also, the moment arm approaches zero when the rotor 
poles approach the stator poles, so the torque drops towards zero twice in 
each revolution. 
To avoid these problems, the "thre e-phase" motor was invented (1 890) in 
which additional electromagnets are located around the circumference, 
rigged to switch on and off approp riately to pull only when the geometry 
gives a good moment arm. To make this happen, the alternating current 
itself has three oscillating sinuso idal waves, superimposed and trans mitted 
on the same wires. 
Some electric motors have even more phases, giving better geomet ry and 
smo other oper ation at the expense of additiona l comp lexity. These can have 
so many different electromagnet locations that the rotor is pulled in its circular path by a magnetic attraction circling around like the fake rabbit that 
dashes around a dog race track, keeping just ahead of the dogs. 
A few poi nts relevant to aircraft design: permanent magnet motors are 
more ef ficient than coil (elect romagnet) motors. Alternating current 
motors require AC power whereas batteries and solar cells are direct 
current, so a converter of some sort is required (us ually as a part of a 
motor controller) . Motors using brushes are typically less efficient, and the 
likel y sparking of brushes is a con cern espec ially with hydrogen fuel cells. 
Brushless motors are usua lly more efficient and reliable, but need sophisticated controllers to electro nically switch the current at the appropriate 
time so they cost a bit more. 
Brushless DC permanent magnet motors (BLDC-P M) are proba bly the 
best choice for aircraft applications given their better efficiency and weight, 
lack of sparking, and good torque characteristi cs. Their reliabil ity and 
safety are likely to be better because the rotating part does not have delicate 
wires. They are used on the Pipistrel Taurus G4, winner of the NASA Green 
Flight Challenge, on the manned electric helicopter, and even on the 
self-balancing Segway Scoot er. 
Electric motors always need a "con troller" of some sort. This can range 
from an on-off switch to a soph isticated-and expen sive-piece of computerdriven high-p ower circ uitry. Even an on-off switch will probably need 
overload protection and some scheme for starting the motor at a reduced 
voltage. Over-temperature sensing and shutoff is always a good idea. 
If the power supply is DC as for batteries, the controller will include a 
DC- AC inverter to produce the final current sent to the motor's electromagnets. For this reason, controllers are some times ref erred to as "inverters. " By 
adjusting the length of time that current is provided during each rotation, the 
speed and power of the motor is controlled.

- Power Suppl y: Batte rie s 
Electric motors are reliable and lightweight. The only problem is that they 
need electrical power, and all of the options weigh a lot more than a tank of 
gasoline . For the ion disc harge test vehicle described above, the power supply 
was a long extension cord. More practical power supplies include batteries, 
fuel cells, solar cells, beamed energy, flywheels, or even a conventional 
engine coupled to an electrical generator, much like a diesel-ele ctric train. 
All have problems, and all suffer from relatively poor energy density. In 
other words, they weigh a lot for the power they can supply over a given 
amount of time. Hope for a fivefold brea kthrough in stored energy 
density-it would change the world. 
Most electric airplanes fly using batteries. The word "battery" actually 
comes from none other than Ben Franklin, who was internation ally famous 
as a leading electrical experimenter long before his involvement in the American Revoluti on. Around 17 50, he was experimenting with Leyden jars, a 
recently invented way to store static electricity and a forerunner of today's

Hybrid- electric power goes back to the 19 30's when trains and submarines began using electric motors powered by diesel engines driving electrical 
generators. In some cases, the diesel engine could also direct ly drive the

avoids the need for clutches and gearing, and provides smoot h torque in 
all conditions. When submerged, hybrid power allows submarines to be 
powered by batteries which are charged by the diesel engine while cruising
